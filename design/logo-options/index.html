What you’re tuning (design science)

  • Accent — primary action color; grabs pre-attentive attention before the user “reads” the UI.
  • Surface vs canvas — figure/ground separation so cards float (Gestalt figure–ground).
  • Text contrast — readability & trust; WCAG suggests ~4.5:1 for body text.
  • Secondary accent — health/nutrition cues without competing with primary CTAs.
  • Radius — soft = approachable consumer; tight = precision / enterprise tool feel. X uses pill radius on CTAs; Facebook/Instagram stay tighter.
  • Social presets — study how red (YouTube) vs black (X) vs blue (Meta/IG actions) changes urgency and “seriousness” on the same layout.

Design theory · logos, color & UX

How these marks and themes relate to user experience through visual perception, cognitive psychology, and product design—not only “what looks cool.” Written for choosing a CookBook system, not a one-off image.

1. Recognition over recall (Nielsen heuristics / cognitive load)

Users should recognize CookBook in a glance on a phone home screen or browser tab. Simple geometric books (#5, #8, #15, #23) minimize detail so the brain matches a known category (“app about cooking/knowledge”) without decoding complex illustration. Dense or textured marks cost milliseconds of processing—small on desktop, painful at 24–32px in a bottom tab bar. Working memory is limited (~4±1 chunks in modern estimates); every extra visual feature competes with meal names, ratings, and CTAs.

Related: Nielsen “Recognition rather than recall”; Sweller cognitive load theory.

2. Gestalt: figure–ground, closure, and dual reads

Clever marks (#9 plate/book, #10 monogram, #19 heart pages, #22 plate-as-book) use closure— the mind completes incomplete shapes—and sometimes dual figure–ground readings. That creates memorability (von Restorff / isolation effect: distinctive items stick) but can hurt first-use clarity if the metaphor is too clever. Enterprise products often prefer single-read icons for trust and scanability; social/consumer apps can afford one clever primary mark if the wordmark always sits nearby.

Gestalt principles (Wertheimer et al.); von Restorff isolation effect.

3. Semantic color, affect, and brand positioning

Color is not decoration—it is a pre-attentive cue. Warm kitchen orange signals “food & comfort” (approach motivation, appetite cues in food marketing research). Cool indigo signals “tools, data, reliability” (common in SaaS and finance). Your enterprise rebrand leans cool so CookBook feels like software you manage inventory and nutrition with—not only a recipe blog. Teal secondary often maps to “health / success” without fighting primary CTAs. Eye-tracking work (Yarbus and later HCI studies) shows contrast and color guide fixations; put accent color only where action or status lives.

Affective design / Kansei; semantic color conventions in UI systems.

4. Visual hierarchy & Fitt’s law (actions people take)

On the mock homepage, the primary button (“Log meal” / “Browse”) uses accent fill so it wins the hierarchy race against muted nav links. That is intentional: users should not hunt for the next step. Fitt’s law says larger, closer targets are faster to hit—so mobile bottom tabs and full-width primary buttons beat tiny icon-only controls for frequent tasks. Logo choice affects hierarchy too: a heavy shield steals attention from content; a quiet geometric mark lets the feed own the page.

5. Consistency & multi-scale systems

A logo must work at 16px (favicon), 32px (nav), and 512px (PWA splash). Outline and flat geometric options scale better than textured shields. Wordmarks (#1, #4, #18, #21) need a companion monochrome icon for constrained contexts—classic logo system practice (primary mark + lockup + monochrome + reverse). Color themes should be tokenized (--accent, --surface…) so light/dark and brand experiments do not require redesigning every component—this lab mirrors that discipline.

6. Trust, credibility, and “enterprise” aesthetics

Shield (#3), badge (#7), and verified check (#16) borrow language from security and certified-data products— useful if you emphasize USDA-backed nutrition. Overusing seals can feel bureaucratic; balance with open layout and whitespace (clutter increases cognitive load and perceived difficulty; white space increases perceived usability in several HCI studies). Data-layer and hex marks (#15, #17, #23) lean “platform / analytics,” which fits Foundation + branded search, but may feel cold for a social kitchen journal—pair with warmer photography and human avatars in the feed.

7. Friendly vs. precise (emotional design levels)

Norman’s three levels of emotional design: visceral (instant like/dislike of shape and color), behavioral (does the product work smoothly?), reflective (identity: “I’m someone who tracks food well”). Steam/soft book (#24) and heart-pages (#19) push visceral warmth; monoline chef-hat book (#20) and stack data (#17) push reflective “serious cook / serious data.” Pick the visceral tone first, then keep behavioral UI consistent so the brand promise matches the product experience.

Don Norman, Emotional Design.

8. How to choose for CookBook UX (practical shortlist)

  • Primary app icon: high silhouette strength — #5, #8, #14, #15, #19, #23, #24.
  • Nav lockup: icon + wordmark — #4, #18, #21.
  • Differentiation: one clever element max (#9, #10, #19, #22), not clever icon and clever wordmark.
  • Science / USDA story: verified or badge language — #7, #16; avoid if the app should feel purely social.
  • Color: pick accent first for CTAs, then surfaces and text contrast—never the reverse.
  • Theme test: use the playground + mock homepage; if chips and buttons disappear into the background, the palette fails hierarchy.

9. Mapping this lab to real product work

What you are doing here is a lightweight design critique loop: generate variants → evaluate against principles → pick a system (icon + lockup + tokens) → implement once in the app. That beats endless AI generations without criteria.
